Salesforce case automation that returned ~2,000 hours/year to the team.

A 6-month internal automation build on top of Salesforce. Replaced manual case triage and resolution flows with a routing layer that handles the common path automatically — letting the team's analysts spend their time on the cases that actually need human judgment.

01The headline

A six-person ops team was spending most of every morning hand-triaging Salesforce cases — assigning owners, routing categories, and closing the obvious resolutions. We replaced the morning ritual with a flow that does the routine work automatically. Return: ~2,000 analyst hours per year, redirected toward the cases that actually require judgment.

02Context & constraints

The team supports Scaled Business Solutions globally. Case volume had grown 3× over two years, but headcount had not. The pain point wasn't that hard cases were getting harder — it was that the team was so submerged in the easy ones, they couldn't get to the hard ones until mid-afternoon.

  • Vendor lock: the work had to live inside Salesforce. No separate orchestration tool.
  • Auditability: every automated decision had to be reviewable by a human and reversible within one click.
  • Scope: the project couldn't change the case data model — only the triage and resolution flows on top of it.
  • Calendar: Q4 freeze landed in month 5; full rollout had to be done before then or wait until Q2.

03The decision

I built the routing layer in two parts: a classifier (rules-based, with a small ML-assisted bucket for the "doesn't match a known pattern" cases) and a resolver (templated responses + auto-close for cases the classifier scored high-confidence on). Everything else got routed to a human with a recommended owner pre-filled.

The most important call wasn't what to automate — it was what not to.

/ What I chose not to do

I didn't try to automate any case the classifier scored below a defensible confidence threshold. The temptation was to ship "auto-close for borderline cases too," and the math said it would have added ~12% more hours saved. But the cost of a wrong auto-close on a real customer issue was an order of magnitude worse than the cost of a human glancing at it. The conservative threshold is why the team trusted the system in month 2 and not month 8.

04Process

Built in four phases. Phase 1 was instrumentation only — we logged every manual decision the team was already making for six weeks. That gave us the ground truth dataset the classifier needed and, more importantly, the answer to "what would this thing have done last quarter?" before we shipped it.

Phase 2 was a shadow-mode rollout — the classifier made its prediction, the human made theirs, and we measured agreement. Phase 3 was a 20% live cutover with daily review. Phase 4 was full cutover with weekly review tapering to monthly.

The thing that almost killed the project was a Salesforce Apex flow limit nobody had hit before. We worked around it; in hindsight, I'd have done the limit math during the spec.

06Tradeoffs & what I'd do differently

/ would do again
Shadow mode before live cutover. The 6-week shadow phase felt slow at the time. In hindsight it was the cheapest insurance policy in the whole project — every classifier rule we'd have shipped without it would have needed a hotfix in week two.
/ would revisit
Apex flow limits. I treated the Salesforce platform constraints as a "we'll find out when we get there" problem. We did, and the workaround was ugly. Spec the limits up front next time.
/ would do differently
Ship the dashboards first, the automation second. We shipped the routing flow before the post-hoc analytics view, which meant for the first six weeks people had to take my word that it was working. With the dashboard first, trust would have built faster.
/ open question
The ML-assisted bucket is opaque. Confidence scores are visible but the model itself is a black box to the operator team. As volume scales, I'd want to swap it for something more interpretable — even if accuracy takes a small hit.
