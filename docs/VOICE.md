# VOICE.md
**Jason Vermaelen — Writing Voice & Style Guide**
_Last updated: May 2026_

> This document captures Jason's natural writing voice, derived from real writing samples including his EMBA capstone business plan, strategy project, accounting analysis, and investor presentation. Use it whenever writing or editing copy for the site to ensure everything sounds like him — not like an AI, a LinkedIn influencer, or a corporate memo.

---

## 1. The Core Voice in Three Words

**Warm. Curious. Grounded.**

Jason writes like someone who has done the thinking and wants to share it clearly — not to show off, but because the ideas actually matter. He's direct without being cold, analytical without being robotic, and thoughtful without being wishy-washy.

---

## 2. Defining Voice Characteristics

### He leads with the "why" before the "what"
Jason doesn't just state conclusions — he builds to them. He sets up context, explains the landscape, then makes his point. Even in formal documents, he takes the reader on a logical journey rather than just dumping a recommendation.

> _Example from his work:_ Before recommending Houston as a growth market, he walked through a full external analysis (PESTEL), then Porter's Five Forces, then internal resources — and only then made the recommendation. The argument earns the conclusion.

**On the site:** Don't start case studies with "Here's what I did." Start with the problem and why it mattered.

---

### He's comfortable with specificity
Jason doesn't say "the company had a lot of debt." He says "the Debt-to-Equity ratio was 0.67 in 2018, falling to 0.63 in 2019." He quantifies, cites sources, and gives exact figures. This is one of the clearest markers of his voice.

> Numbers aren't decoration — they're the point.

**On the site:** Back every claim with data. If a number is unavailable, explain why the outcome mattered in concrete terms.

---

### He acknowledges tradeoffs and uncertainty honestly
Jason doesn't oversell. He'll identify a weakness in a company's financials, note a risk in a business plan, or point out when something "needs some work." This intellectual honesty is part of what makes his writing trustworthy.

> _Example from his work:_ "While the company's liquidity and solvency look very good, it does need some work on profitability."

**On the site:** Don't write like everything always worked perfectly. Acknowledging challenges and what you learned from them is more compelling than a highlight reel.

---

### He writes in plain, direct sentences
Jason avoids jargon and over-complicated sentence structures. His paragraphs are focused — usually one main idea per paragraph, explained clearly, then moved on from. He doesn't pad for length.

> He'll write "this looks to be a prime time to consider taking loans for more growth" rather than "the current macroeconomic environment presents a compelling opportunity to capitalize on favorable financing conditions."

**On the site:** If a sentence sounds like it belongs in a McKinsey deck, rewrite it.

---

### He has a quiet confidence
Jason makes recommendations and owns them. He doesn't hedge everything with "perhaps" or "it might be worth considering." He states his view and explains why.

> _Example from his work:_ "My recommendation is to expand to the city of Houston, TX for a few reasons."

**On the site:** Write with conviction. State what you did, what you believed, and what happened. Don't bury the point.

---

### He's practical and solution-oriented
Jason doesn't just identify problems — he immediately moves toward solutions. In his accounting analysis, he identified profitability issues and went straight into concrete recommendations (renegotiate the Spencer's commission, invest in distilling equipment).

**On the site:** Every case study should end with either a result or a lesson — something that moved things forward.

## 3. Tone by Section

| Section | Tone |
|---|---|
| **Hero / Home** | Confident, human, clear. One idea. No jargon. |
| **About** | Warm and personal. First person. Tell a real story. |
| **Case Studies** | Analytical but accessible. Lead with business context, not tech. |
| **Blog Posts** | Conversational and curious. Like explaining something interesting to a smart friend. |
| **Resume / Experience** | Crisp and action-oriented. Active verbs, specific outcomes. |
| **Contact** | Short. Friendly. No corporate speak. |

---

## 4. Words & Phrases That Sound Like Jason

These are patterns pulled from his actual writing:

- "Let's look at…" / "Now that we have X, let's…" — he guides the reader forward
- "For a few reasons" — he lists and explains, doesn't just assert
- "While X looks good, Y needs some work" — honest framing of tradeoffs
- "This means…" — he connects data points to meaning explicitly
- "To help [company/person] manage this…" — practical, not theoretical
- Short declarative sentences after a longer explanation: _"So currently, the company is not very profitable from a net perspective."_
- He frames the "so what" clearly: _"This means for each barrel sold, the company is only netting 2%."_

---

## 5. Words & Phrases to Avoid

These do **not** sound like Jason:

| Avoid | Why |
|---|---|
| _Leverage_ (as a verb) | Overused corporate filler |
| _Synergies_ | Vague and buzzword-y |
| _Robust_ | Meaningless without context |
| _Excited to share_ | LinkedIn energy |
| _Passionate about_ (as opener) | Overused; show it through the work instead |
| _Utilize_ | Just say "use" |
| _Best-in-class_ | Unless backed by a specific claim |
| _Thought leader_ | Never, ever |
| _Circle back / touch base_ | Corporate filler |
| _I am a results-driven professional_ | Resume cliché |

## 6. Structural Patterns

**How Jason opens a piece of writing:**
He tends to open by setting the context or problem clearly — not with a hook or a provocative question. He trusts the reader to care if the problem is stated clearly.

> Bad: "Have you ever wondered why so many Americans can't afford a home?"
> Good: "As home prices continue to rise and median wages remain well below home costs, buying a home is becoming further out of reach for many Americans."

**How Jason ends a piece of writing:**
He ends with a clear conclusion or next step — not a summary of what was already said. He doesn't say "in conclusion" or "as we can see." He just makes the final point and stops.

**Paragraph length:**
Medium. Not one-liners, not walls of text. Usually 3–5 sentences. He develops an idea fully before moving on.

---

## 7. Voice Checklist

Before publishing any piece of content, ask:

- [ ] Does it lead with context or a problem, not a conclusion?
- [ ] Are there specific numbers or examples to back up key claims?
- [ ] Is it written in plain language — no jargon a non-expert would need to Google?
- [ ] Does it acknowledge at least one tradeoff, risk, or honest limitation?
- [ ] Does it sound like a smart, curious person — not a press release?
- [ ] Are the sentences direct and the paragraphs focused?
- [ ] Is every word earning its place, or is something padding?
